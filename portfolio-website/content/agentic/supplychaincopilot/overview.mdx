## The problem

Three mid-market manufacturers were each absorbing about two weeks of
supplier delay per critical part, every quarter. The pattern was always
the same: a tier-2 supplier slipped, the buyer found out late, and the
line was already shorting components by the time procurement had a
substitute lined up. Stockouts were recurring rather than exceptional.

## Constraints

The ERP was non-negotiable. Two of the three customers had finished
migrations to it within the last eighteen months and were not going to
replace anything sitting under it — not the master data, not the PO
workflow, and not the audit trail that compliance had signed off on.
The other hard constraint was traceability: every recommendation the
system made had to be defensible after the fact, with the inputs
referenced and the model output preserved. "Because the AI said so"
was not an acceptable answer for a $400k order.

## Who used it

Procurement managers — three of them across the three customers, with
buyers picking up the routine cases. The managers reviewed only the
flagged decisions where the system either had low confidence or where
the recommended action exceeded a spend threshold. Everything else
went through straight from the buyer queue.

## What the system does

Three specialist agents share a shared scratchpad and negotiate a
single recommendation per flagged purchase order:

- The **inventory agent** reads stock levels, open POs, and the demand
  forecast for the affected part family. Its job is to surface "do we
  actually need a replacement, and by when".
- The **supplier agent** ranks alternates against the original supplier
  on lead time, qualified-supplier status, price delta, and recent
  on-time performance. It pulls from the SQL supplier master plus a
  vector index of three years of QA notes.
- The **decision agent** synthesizes both, writes a recommendation in
  the procurement manager's voice, and attaches the evidence chain.

The output is a one-screen brief: recommended action, projected impact,
the alternatives that were considered and rejected, and a link back to
every source the recommendation depended on. The procurement manager
either approves, edits, or rejects — and the system learns from the
rejection reason rather than the rejection itself.
